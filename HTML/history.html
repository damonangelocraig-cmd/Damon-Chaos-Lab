A Brief History of Chaos Theory

Chaos theory developed through discoveries in mathematics, physics, and weather science. This timeline introduces the people and ideas that changed how we understand complex systems.

Origins of Chaos

The foundation behind chaos theory can be seen as early as the late 1600s, when the idea of “determinism” began to emerge. However, at this point in time, concepts behind determinism and chaos were philosophical and lacked the mathematical and scientific grounds behind them.

Pierre-Simon Laplace

  • French mathematician and astronomer whose deterministic view of the universe influenced the development of chaos theory. However, the emergence of chaos theory was the antithesis of Laplace’s belief.
  • Laplace's Demon: Imagine an absolute omniscient being. If it knows the position/momentum of every particle in the universe, it could determine the future or rewrite the past (based on the initial conditions of the universe).
  • Determinism: You can predict the future and past using current initial conditions. However, this states that the future and past are predetermined, and that free will is not a real thing.
  • It is important to note that Laplace’s philosophy fails according to modern physics. Quantum mechanics proves that systems are not strictly deterministic but are more probabilistic, and Heisenberg's uncertainty principle makes it impossible to know both the precise momentum and position of a particle.

The Butterfly Effect

  • Being one of the most well-known ideas derived from Chaos Theory, the Butterfly Effect talks about the varying impacts, or different outcomes, produced by a system when small changes are made to its initial conditions.
  • Discoveries regarding chaos theory are typically attributed to Edward Lorenz, an American mathematician/meteorologist whose work on weather predictions revealed the concept behind the butterfly effect (that changes to initial conditions change the product of a system).

Lorenz and the Royal McBee

  • Holding an interest in weather prediction, Lorenz wished to use his expertise in both meteorology and mathematics to find set equations for weather predictions, each calculating changes according to different external variables like pressure, wind speed, temperature, and more.
  • In the 1960s, Lorenz’s experiments would result in the creation of Lorenz’s 12-variable model, a system of 12 different differential equations which are influenced by external variables like the ones mentioned above. Its functionality was seemingly accurate, producing weather conditions that changed constantly and were, to some degree, chaotic, but still had a structure and order.
  • This changed in winter 1961, when Lorenz wished to observe a portion of a sequence at a larger length, but used a shortcut to do so, using numbers from a random point in the middle of the sequence as the initial conditions for the machine. He then left for an hour-long coffee break… When he returned, everything had changed.
  • It is important to note that this all occurred at the advent of computer usage. Thus, Lorenz’s first thought was that it was an issue with his computer, the Royal McBee. However, it was soon realized that the real issue lay in the initial conditions he plugged in. The computer’s calculations were running according to the sixth decimal place; however, its results were only being printed to the third due to space on its page and were thus only plugged in to the third decimal for the new sequence.

Final Outcome

  • The large influence of small changes to a system was eventually coined as the Butterfly Effect, the sensitive dependence on initial conditions in which a small change in one state of a deterministic nonlinear system can result in large differences in a later state.
  • It is important to note that people previously viewed these unpredictable changes in behavior as incorrect, discarding them as an error of a deterministic system. Lorenz was the first to realize that the randomness had significance.
  • The 12-variable model was soon shortened to 3, commonly known as the Lorenz Attractor, an infinite loop resembling a butterfly that falls within certain bounds but will never repeat itself.
